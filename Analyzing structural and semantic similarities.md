#### Background

**Large language models in business process management** Several publications have explored and described potential use cases and scenarios involving tools, such as GPT-4 (OpenAI 2023), in BPM. Thanks to their ability to understand both structured and unstructured input, LLMs are basically capable of performing a variety of tasks in BPM, ranging from process discovery (Norouzifar et al. 2024; Berti and Qafari 2023) and model generation (Kourani et al. 2024, 2025; Vidgof et al. 2023; Bennoit et al. 2024), providing process-based information in various output formats (Vidgof et al. 2023; Bennoit et al. 2024; Estrada-Torres et al. 2024) to monitoring KPIs and recommending possible process bottlenecks (Vidgof et al. 2023; Estrada-Torres et al. 2024; Grohs et al. 2023). Therefore, it seems that this technology could be beneficial for the BPM user groups, like Process Analysts, Process Methodists, System Engineers or Process Consultants, in their day-to-day work (Dumas et al. 2021; Scheer and Brabänder

2010). **Interacting with large language models via prompts** Interaction with LLMs takes place through verbal or textual input, or other trig- ger events. When providing input to an LLM, the definition of prompts and prompting structures may help improve reliability and replicability of outputs. In this context, prompt engineering can be defined as the practice of structuring and refining input instructions to guide LLMs toward producing desired outputs. Research shows that specific prompting strategies, such as chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023), can significantly improve reasoning performance, while other techniques focus on reducing hallucinations and improving reliability (Si et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024). Several studies in the area of BPM have explored ways to interact effectively with an LLM. For example, (Fill et al. 2023) used LLMs to understand, alter and output process information using semi-structured text formats, like JSON or XML, showing promising results. The authors were able to create models in various notations (like Entity Rela- tionship Diagrams or Unified Modelling Language diagrams) from textual descriptions using such formats. Consequently, they argue that standardized methods and evaluation metrics should be defined to further improve output quality (Fettke and Houy 2025). The study of (Kourani et al. 2024) comes to a similar conclusion, using abstraction techniques like XML or JSON. By defining a framework, (Kourani et al. 2025) explored the possibilities of LLMs to automatically generate and iteratively refine process models starting from textual descriptions. They instantiated a system to extend their framework

The authors were able to demonstrate an interactive process model generation flow. Overall, this suggests that prompt engineering and the usage of semi-structured formats, like JSON, seem to be important factors in ensuring reliable and accurate outputs. Therefore, in the frame of this test scenario, a semi-structured format will be used as well. When using LLMs in the BPM context, another interesting aspect is their ability to accurately understand process flows, semantics, and relationships within a formal business process model correctly. This reverse mapping indicates the LLMs’ capability to genuinely grasp process flows and relationships, being a good indicator for using them to analyze formal business process models as input.

#### Related works

**Comparing process models with one another** By comparing process models with one another, it becomes possible to identify similarities that support tasks such as checking for consistency and completeness checking, or bridging the gap between business needs and IT implementation. As defined earlier, reference models are standardized, best-practice, sector-specific models that help companies adapt their processes for such particular purposes (Dumas et al. 2021; Küster et al. 2006). Traditionally, one approach is to make slight adjustments to the reference models to match a company’s specific needs, while still preserving their intended value, and then implement them accordingly (Dumas et al. 2021). Another approach is to compare reference models with a company’s formal business process models and merge them into a new model, retaining certain parts while replacing others. Such a systematic model-merging approach is described by (Küster et al. 2006). In this context, several BPM use cases were described by (Aalst 2013), like the merging of process models, composing multiple models into one bigger model, or comparing two or more models to verify the logical correctness and completeness and adapt processes. When comparing process models, the question of which similarity metrics to use arises. By performing a systematic literature review, (Schoknecht et al. 2017) identified four main similarity metrics, namely *natural language* used to describe elements, *graph* *structure* describing the order of activities and connectors, *process behavior* analyzing the execution of processes, and *human estimation* considering humans’ knowledge. As an example, (Dijkman et al. 2011) defined and validated a set of similarity metrics to detect semantic (e.g., the labeling of elements), structural (e.g., the relations between elements), and behavioral deviations (i.e., the intended behavior of a process) across various modeling notations by introducing abstracted business process graphs. Here, they proposed five parameters to measure similarity between elements, including label syn- tax and semantics, attribute and node type similarity, as well as the overall contextual

Bennoit *et al. Process Science* (2026) 3:22 Page 5 of 20

similarity of the process flow. Their results show that this framework outperforms purely text-based search engines in identifying similarities between process models in databases. Similar to (Schoknecht et al. 2017; Dumas et al. 2021) and (Zampino et al. 2025) find that *structural* (syntactic modeling notation accuracy), *semantic* (natural language aspects), and *behavioral similarities* are key metrics, with (Zampino et al. 2025) plac- ing particular emphasis on the latter. They outline evaluation metrics such as runtime behavior analysis using trace-based approaches to compare the actual execution traces captured in event logs with a process model, or conformance checking. Closely related to this, another approach to process comparison, is the use of Process Mining methods. Based on event log data generated during process execution, Process Mining reconstructs the actual process flow as it occurs in reality. By comparing these reconstructed flows with a company’s originally defined process models, deviations between the planned and the actual execution of the process can be identified. In particular, syntactic deviations, such as recorded or skipped activities, as well as process quality parameters, like potential bottlenecks and weaknesses in the process can be detected (Dumas et al. 2021; Aalst 2013; Van Der Aalst and Carmona 2022). LLMs could serve as an accessible, easy-to-use tool for relevant user groups, as they require neither programming skills nor specialized terminology. They may help detect not only syntactic but also semantic deviations quickly, enabling them to distinguish purely terminological differences, such as synonyms, from actual process deviations. When provided with process models derived from event logs, LLMs could also compare them with the intended to-be-processes to analyze behavioral deviations. Beyond that, their broad world knowledge could allow LLMs to incorporate expert perspectives on specific processes, thereby supporting human estimation metrics.

**Examination and evaluation of process models based on specific criteria** The strengths of LLMs to process natural language, as outlined before, could offer meaningful application possibilities in various areas of BPM. In particular, the examination and evaluation of process models based on specific criteria, such as comparing them with a reference process, may represent a suitable use case. Several studies have concep- tually outlined this use case. For example, (Vidgof et al. 2023) describe the use of LLMs for issue discovery and spotting by using them to analyze task names and descriptions to make suggestions for possible issues. (Klievtsova et al. 2024) also describe the use of LLMs for analyzing syntactic and semantic correctness of formal business process models. Similar suggestions were also made by (Estrada-Torres et al. 2024; Buss et al. 2025; Kampik et al. 2024, 2025) describe the use of LLMs in combination with Process Mining techniques to enhance process observability and enable continuous process monitoring, analysis, and adaption.

This chapter outlines the framework for the methodological approach used to investigate the central research question stated in the instruction. To answer this, the study was designed around a controlled comparison framework. The core idea is to provide the LLM with a correct reference model and one or more modified variants of that model. These variants were deliberately altered to contain either typical modeling mistakes or

**Tool selection** For the course of this paper, the **ARIS** tool was utilized as one of the most established enterprise-level tools for business process modeling. It is widely adopted in both aca- demic and industrial settings and supports a broad array of modeling standards. Additionally, we decided to use the latest version of OpenAI’s ChatGPT, based on the **GPT-5.1 thinking architecture** as LLM, released in November 2025. This decision was guided by recent evaluations that consistently highlight the strong performance of ChatGPT in reasoning and error-detection tasks across different domains (Li et al. 2024; Naveed et al. 2025; Minaee et al. 2024). Beyond its demonstrated accuracy, ChatGPT was also selected because it is one of the most widely used and accessible LLMs, making it particularly relevant for assessing the practical applicability of such approaches in real-world modeling environments.

**Process model selection** With the modeling tool and LLM selected, the next step involved the creation of test cases that could be used to evaluate the ability of the LLM to identify deviations between formal business process models. ***Structural parameters*** Referring to (Dumas et al. 2021; Schoknecht et al. 2017; Zampino et al. 2025), structural parameters focus on the syntactic correctness of the process modeling notation used to describe the processes under analysis. Further refining the notion of structural parameters, (Dumas et al. 2021) differenti- ate between *structural rules themselves*, which concern the relations between elements, and *execution rules*, which relate to the correct execution of the process flow. Structural rules can be quantified, for example, by the number and position of elements in the model, the order of activities and connectors (Schoknecht et al. 2017), or the correct use of sequence flows (e.g., every activity must have at least one incoming or outgoing sequence flow, start-events can not have incoming sequence flows) (Dumas et al. 2021).

**Result evaluation** The primary output objective was a comprehensive comparison of the models, focusing on whether the LLM could accurately identify all incorporated modifications without introducing false positives. While no strict output format was enforced, tabular summa- ries indicating detected issues and categorizing the type of metrics (e.g., missing state, incorrect gateway) appear to be the most effective for evaluation purposes. To assess the effectiveness of *GPT-5.1 thinking* in identifying differences between formal business process models, the evaluation was based on a comparison between the model’s output and the deliberately incorporated modifications within the test cases. Each prompt response was analyzed to determine whether the LLM correctly detected the intended discrepancies while avoiding the introduction of false positives. To ensure the robustness and consistency of the results, each test for each model, modification, and comparison method was executed **five times**. This repetition of test runs aimed to minimize the possibility of coincidental success and to verify the reliability of the LLM’s behavior across repeated interactions. Since LLM outputs are probabilis- tic, as described above, additional factors such as model parameters (e.g., temperature) and user-specific settings may influence the generated responses. We therefore aimed to assess whether missed or misinterpreted modifications can be identified reliably. More specifically, we examined whether an error made by the LLM during model comparison occurs consistently across repeated test runs or only occasionally. Because the prompt remained unchanged throughout the testing, this may allow conclusions to be drawn about the consistency and, to a limited extent, the reliability of the LLM’s behavior. The results for all three testing scenarios (single modification, multiple modification, batch processing) were classified into three categories: **Full accuracy**: *All* of the following cases are accomplished during *all* test runs:

- The LLM successfully detected all intentionally introduced modifications in every model,
- The LLM did not report any additional, non-existent modifications, and
- The LLM did not start to hallucinate any (made up) information about the models.
**Partial accuracy:** *One of* the following cases occurred during *one of* the test runs:

- The LLM did not identify one modification injected correctly in any model,
- the LLM reported one non-existent modification, or

- More than once, the LLM did not correctly identify one or more modification injected in one or more models,

#### Test results

**Single modification per model for complexity pools 1, 2, and 3** All test cases from **every complexity pool** were evaluated correctly and completely error-free on the first attempt. The LLM was able to detect every introduced modification without any mistakes, no non-existing modifications were reported, and no hallucinations occurred. Each time, the LLM provided a highly detailed table of identified modifications, clearly indicating where each deviation occurred. All structural parameters were assessed correctly. Regarding the semantic parameters, the LLM’s sensitivity to wording differences could be demonstrated: When activity labels were rephrased but retained the same functional meaning, the LLM indeed listed them in its results table as deviations, but explicitly noted that they were not “incorrect”, and explaining that both elements convey the same meaning. The LLM gave as output: “Label changed to *Request a Price Quote*, same type and lane, same position in the sequence. Treated as semantically equivalent to *Request a Cost Estimation*.” Even without any additional helper prompts, the LLM distinguished Metrics 10 and 11 flawlessly. Concluding, the LLM achieved **full accuracy** in this setup.

**Multiple modifications in complexity pool 3** Building on the results of the single modification tests, the outcomes of the multiple modification scenarios also revealed a clear pattern. In all cases where the LLM had previously identified each individual deviation in isolation, it was likewise able to detect two or four modifications when they appeared together in a single model. For both structural and semantic parameters, the LLM listed all deviations and precisely indicated where they occurred. This suggests that the presence of multiple modifications did not impair the LLM’s ability to recognize individual deviations and demonstrates its capability to

Bennoit *et al. Process Science* (2026) 3:22 Page 14 of 20

process compound modifications. As with the single modification tests, no additional helper prompts were required. Concluding, the LLM achieved **full accuracy** in this setup as well.

**Batch processing of multiple models** The previous results demonstrated that *GPT-5.1 thinking* was capable of successfully identifying multiple modifications within a single model. The third testing scenario aimed to analyze the LLM’s ability to process multiple models containing one or more modifications simultaneously. The type of modification was selected at random; the sole focus was on whether all modifications were still correctly detected. To ensure the results were sufficiently comprehensive, the total number of modifications across all provided models was controlled by consistently including one model with four modifications and all remaining models with just a single modification each. This resulted in the following numbers of modifications: 5 (two altered models), 6 (three altered models), 7 (four altered models), and 12 (nine altered models). We made this choice based on the previously convincing results of our testing. The aim of this setup is to evaluate whether the LLM continues to identify all modifications correctly or whether its reliability decreases. First, one reference model from each complexity pool was provided to the LLM along with two modified models (five modifications in total). In all test runs, the LLM contin- ued to reliably detect every modification and clearly identify its exact location. When three modified models were provided (six modifications in total), we began to observe a decline in the level of detail with which the LLM reported the deviations. Although it still detected all modifications reliably and did not report any false positives, it no lon- ger specified the exact location of each deviation and started to summarize metrics into groups like “significant deviations”. Instead, the LLM stated that it had identified deviations and listed them without further elaboration. The LLM began to overlook modifications when four models were provided (seven modifications in total). As already observed with three models, the level of detail in the LLM’s results table decreased. In addition, across all test runs, the LLM consistently missed zero to two modifications, although the specific overlooked modification varied between runs. Nevertheless, the model never began to hallucinate or report deviations that did not exist. In one run, the model identified all modifications correctly. In three runs, it failed to correctly identify the modifications associated with Metrics 1, 2, and 9. In one run, the model simultaneously missed two modifications (Metric 7 and 9). Additionally, all modifications missing from the LLM’s results table were either injected into models from complexity pool 3 or occurred in models containing four combined modifications. Throughout the entire batch processing test scenario, modifications in models from complexity pool 1 and 2 were consistently identified accurately. The same pattern was observed when nine models were provided (twelve modifications in total), although the number of missed modifications increased to as many as three. None of the test runs was free of missed modifications. In four test runs, two metrics were missed in varying combinations (Metrics 1 and 2; Metrics 2 and 4; Metrics 2 and 7; Metrics 4 and 7). In one run, three Metrics were missed (Metrics 2, 3, and 9). In conclusion, the LLM achieved **full accuracy** in this setup when processing up to three models simultaneously, containing up to six modifications. However, the

decreasing level of detail in its outputs may already indicate a decline in overall quality. Accuracy dropped to **poor** when more than three models (or more than six modifications) were provided at the same time.

**Conclusions** **What are the results of using a Large Language Model to detect deviations in formal business process models by comparing modified models with their original reference models within a specific modeling notation?**

To address this question, three structured testing scenarios were developed and executed to examine the accuracy and reliability of the selected LLM’s performance. Overall, the results indicate that *GPT-5.1 thinking* is capable of identifying a wide range of modeling modifications, both semantic and syntactic in nature. For the testing scenarios, a single standardized and relatively simple prompt was designed. It clearly specified the comparison task and included examples of modifications. The models themselves were provided in a semi-structured, textual format (native BPMN .bpmn files). Testing scenario one (comparing the reference model with a model containing a single modification) and testing scenario two (comparing the reference model with a model containing either two or four combined modifications) demonstrated that the LLM can reliably detect all deviations across every defined metric class. It was able to correctly interpret natural language to identify elements with different labels that represent the same content. Similarly, graphical layout variations, such as repositioning elements or reordering lanes, did not affect detection accuracy. These findings underscore two key advantages of LLM-based evaluation: its abstraction from visual representation, which is often a major challenge in manual comparison, and accurate interpretation of natural language. Together, these features highlight the LLM’s suitability for formal business process model comparison and validation tasks. However, reliability decreased in testing scenario 3, where the LLM was simultaneously provided with the reference model and multiple altered models containing various

Bennoit *et al. Process Science* (2026) 3:22 Page 17 of 20

modifications. When more than three models were supplied for comparison (that is, more than six modifications in total), the LLM began to overlook modifications across different metric classes. The frequency of missing modifications across all test runs in this batch processing setup resulted in a standard deviation of 1.065 (arithmetic mean of

0.143) for seven modifications (provision of four modified models simultaneously) and
2.056 (arithmetic mean of 0.183) for twelve modifications (provision of nine modified models simultaneously), indicating a wide spread and considerable uncertainty in the correct and complete identification of modifications. The LLM was tested up to the point at which its precision began to decline, leaving room for interpretation as to why this occurred. For instance, the process of injecting certain modifications may require further evaluation to determine how it influences the LLM’s precision. In addition, further guidance through prompt adjustment or supplementary helper prompts might be required. In conclusion, the ability of the selected LLM to identify deviations in formal business process models by comparing them with their respective reference model was demonstrated, showing high accuracy. However, the results also revealed its potential limitations when multiple models are provided simultaneously, with accuracy declining to poor under these conditions.
#### Limitations and implications for future work

While the results presented in this study demonstrate that *GPT-5.1 thinking* can successfully detect and interpret differences in formal business process models in BPMN (.bpmn) format, several aspects offer promising directions for future research: First, additional LLMs should be evaluated. This study relied solely on *GPT-5.1 thinking*, which already showed high accuracy in identifying modifications. Future work could compare its performance with further, newer model architectures to determine whether similar or improved results can be achieved. Building on this, advancements in prompt engineering or model fine-tuning may further enhance accuracy and reduce the need for iterative interaction. Second, alternative process modeling notation languages should be explored. We used BPMN, given its widespread adoption as industry standard. Examining other notations, such as Event-Driven Process Chains, Unified Modeling Language, or Entity Relation- ship models, may provide additional insights into an LLM’s capabilities from different modeling perspectives and domains. Additionally, increasing the diversity and complexity of the models compared could yield deeper insights. Future work could incorporate larger or more complex models, or broaden the number of models analyzed, to examine how well the approach scales. Thirdly, related to this, more efficient strategies for using LLMs in model comparison should be investigated. This study introduced batch processing as an initial attempt, but there remains significant potential for optimization, such as handling larger batches or enabling selective comparison, while keeping the user effort minimal. The LLM began to overlook modifications when analyzing more than three models simultaneously, highlighting the need for further analysis to determine the cause. Also, the number of repeti- tions, which we set to five, can be increased to collect more statistical data. Fourth, this paper focused on structural and semantic similarity parameters. The findings encourage further research on behavioral similarities as well as human estimation

Bennoit *et al. Process Science* (2026) 3:22 Page 18 of 20

parameters. For example, an LLM’s extensive world knowledge could potentially be used to enrich process models with domain-specific knowledge. Additionally, expanding the set of modifications used, introducing more combinations, or systematically testing which metrics tend to cause errors more likely would extend the applicability of the findings to more complicated or less common scenarios. Although this would increase the number of test cases and the complexity of evaluation, it would provide a deeper understanding of the limitations and strengths of LLM-based analysis. Closely related, the process of injecting modifications into the process models may require further investigation, as it could represent a source of declining precision in the LLM’s ability to identify modifications. Fifth, we did not further examine the impact of the language used to perform the tests. Throughout the course of this research, it became apparent that language does not seem to influence interactions with the LLM. Whether the model was prompted in English or German, the results remained unchanged. This aspect may open up additional avenues for research, as overcoming language barriers continues to be an unsolved challenge for many (international) companies. Lastly, integrating LLMs with other existing process comparison techniques, such as Directly Follows Graphs sourced from event logs in the context of process mining serving as process variants for comparison, may unlock even more powerful and practical application scenarios.
